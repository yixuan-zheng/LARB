# Predictor Design and Evaluation Review

**Recommendation:** Start with a CPU audit that separates ranking from calibration on archived, repeatedly sampled outcomes. Give a cheap question-level predictor one prespecified test. Compare it with an early-reasoning signal only where the saved artifacts support a fair paired comparison. If neither offers a credible advantage after overhead, put prediction aside and prioritize load-aware scheduling. The current evidence supports testing budget allocation on the workload, while the tested predictors have yet to earn a serving trial.

## 1. What LARB should predict

| Target | Definition | Scheduling value and limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Question difficulty | Probability of failure at a specified reference budget, or an external difficulty rating. | A cheap baseline. Hard questions can be unsalvageable, and moderately difficult questions may offer the largest gain from more work. |
| Minimum sufficient budget | Smallest budget at which estimated correctness probability crosses an explicit threshold, such as 75%. | Useful under a per-request quality target. Some questions never cross the threshold. With two seeds, the first observed success is a noisy statistic and may disappear on resampling. |
| Marginal reasoning benefit | Signed difference in expected correctness for a particular transition, such as `p(6K) − p(4K)`. | Closest to the scheduler's decision. It distinguishes expensive improvements from low-value extensions and allows for regressions. Its estimation is noisier than estimating correctness at one budget. |

Marginal benefit is the right **value input** when the decision is how much compute to spend on one request instead of another. For deadline goodput, the actual value is the change in **probability of a correct answer by the deadline**, including the extra delay imposed on other requests. A scheduler still needs measured incremental cost, queue pressure and deadline slack. A cheap difficulty score may outperform a direct benefit forecast if its out-of-sample ranking is more stable. Compare both on the same decision and cost. At admission, estimate differences between independent budget policies. At a live checkpoint, estimate differences conditional on the same still-open prefix. These are distinct quantities.

## 2. Evidence and plausible failure modes

| Predictor | Information and prediction | What the evidence establishes |
|---|---|---|
| Archived question-only and full-prefix readers | Question-only forecasts are shared by the seeds of a question. Prefix readers see the question and current reasoning and forecast short/long outcomes; the allocation score is predicted wrong-to-right probability minus right-to-wrong probability. | Week 6 full-prefix rankings gained four expected answers per 57 at matched realized cost with reading treated as free. Cost was unmeasured. The 27B full-prefix reader's equal-extension advantage over its own question-only control went from +6 on Week 6 to −1 on Week 7. Measured reader cost was well above estimated headroom. There was some ranking information on development data, with weak transfer and poor economics. |
| Bounded and recoverable-step readers | The bounded reader used the question and last 512 tokens at the 4K checkpoint, scoring outcome classes from first-token logits. The step reader asked whether a concrete repair could be shown and combined that classification with a question-only rank. | Bounded prefix got 22/57 at 28 extensions versus 25/57 for the same reader on the question alone. Its within-question direction was right in one of four discordant questions. The step reader lost 25/60 to 26/60 at the charged ceiling. Its audit found frequent malformed/truncated replies and unsupported proposed steps. This implicates both format and ranking in those implementations. |
| Hidden-state probes | A frozen predictor read internal features at 4K. Follow-ups tried a nonlinear readout and changes in features from 2K to 4K. | Protected validation found +2.24 points versus prompt-only with a 95% interval of −0.15 to +4.98 and 46.1% within-question concordance. | Qwen3-4B model-native selector | Question text only, non-thinking chat prompt, greedy decoding, eight output tokens. The prompt changes demonstrably shifted the selector's operating point. The original asked for the smallest sufficient tier with no reliability threshold or empirical calibration. The rubric placed unfamiliar or complex work near the top tier. A hard decoder fixes syntax and greedy decoding discards runner-up information. Reversing the choice list weakens a simple first-choice decoder explanation. It leaves open a verbal-label prior, a model that cannot interpret the absolute token scale, and a model with some latent ranking obscured by the final label. Neither prompt result shows whether continuous scores have useful ordering. The few-shot variant only had qualifying 2K and 4K examples, so it is weak evidence about calibration across all four caps. The lowest cap also hit the answer allowance 11 times, which contributes to the measured policy difference under the current contract. On the selector-audit split, length ranking scored 18/32 at 6,180 mean total tokens versus an expected 15.5/32 at 6,106 for balanced random allocation. Its paired interval spans a loss. Use the grid for diagnostics and baseline replay. Its two seeds cannot establish a per-question success probability or a stable minimum tier. ## 3. Candidate designs

| Design | Information and output | Cost and integration |
|---|---|---|
| Calibrated question-only baseline | Use length and one frozen available question score to estimate correctness by cap with a small regularized model or coarse shrinkage bins. Derive signed differences. Compare with direct difficulty and global mean gain. Learn calibration only on disjoint questions and use archived scores only where their collection is comparable. | Length is available at admission with near-zero overhead. An already cached score is cheap for replay. Fresh scoring must pay its online cost on every request. Simple LARB admission integration. |
| One-call relative benefit rank | For one transition, ask whether extra reasoning improves the chance of a correct answer, and save the continuous logit margin from two verified single-token choices. Compare with a question-only difficulty score from the same model and budget. Test label order or polarity on development questions, then freeze it. Keep raw ranking alongside a calibrated gain. | One short prefill and one decision token, no explanation generation. More costly than length and still exposed to queueing. Top-k order survives monotone calibration, which helps separate ranking ability from threshold choice. |
| Cheap early-reasoning increment | Add one prespecified feature already present in saved short prefixes or telemetry, such as repeated n-gram fraction, conditional on still being open at the chosen checkpoint. Compare question-only with question plus feature over the exact same prefixes. | CPU replay first. Online cost should be limited to incremental bookkeeping, subject to measurement. Limited coverage and an engine hook make this a conditional next step. Avoid a new sequence reader. |

For the initial 4K→8K screen, sort by predicted signed gain per expected incremental cost and compare at predeclared operating points. For all four tiers, choose the cap maximizing `predicted correctness − λ × predicted total cost` over all feasible caps, with a queue-aware price and deadline constraints specified separately. Evaluating all four choices handles negative or nonmonotone increments. Freeze estimated costs and the allocation rule before held-out outcomes are opened. Use realized costs afterward for scoring.

## 4. Evaluation and staged execution

1. **CPU evidence audit.** Inventory question IDs, prefixes, seeds, model pins, costs and actual score availability. Keep all variants of one question together and check benchmark overlap. Use the existing Omni-MATH split for retrospective diagnosis and the archived checkpoint draws for repeated-outcome analysis. Freeze disjoint development, calibration and evaluation question lists before fitting anything. Previously inspected cohorts remain exploratory. A calibration subset can fit a simple intercept/slope or bins. Evaluation questions must remain untouched by prompt, feature, and cutoff choices. Reuse saved prompt-only and prefix scores on common cohorts. Use the existing scoring scripts for grouped replay. No GPU allocation is needed for this stage. [Cost-matched scoring protocol](../../../week7/scoring/PROTOCOL.md)
2. **Separate ranking from calibration.** On common outcomes, report raw score order, gain by score bin, signed pairwise concordance, and calibration of predicted correctness at each cap. Compare raw and calibrated scores with global mean gain, reference-budget difficulty, length and the archived question-only score. Recompute grouped intervals by question, keeping seeds together. For early features also compare siblings on questions with different branch gains. Track coverage and distinguish between-question selection from within-question ranking. Check the first and second halves of repeated draws for label stability. A calibration fix can improve estimated gains and operating thresholds while leaving the ranking unchanged. A flat ranking after polarity and syntax checks is evidence against the tested readout.
3. **Score allocation independently of serving.** Include each fixed cap, question length, budget-histogram-matched shuffle, mixtures of fixed caps, and comparable question-only predictors. Report accuracy at matched realized token cost and cost at equal accuracy. Record prefill, output, feature extraction, latency and amortization assumptions separately. Use measured accelerator time when available. Report gains captured, gains missed, regressions selected, deadline-risk exposure, and a retrospective oracle as an upper bound. Diagnose disagreements with examples across easy ceiling, recoverable, all-wrong, harmful extension, and answer-capped cases. Include prompt-label prior checks and separate changes in solver correctness from changes in answer-cap frequency. Keep any later live serving test separate, with paired arrival traces and correct-on-time completions as its outcome.
4. **Smallest new measurement, conditional on CPU evidence.** First score the original label prior on neutral prompts and collect one relative 4K→8K score per existing question on a single L40S. Use the existing 8 development questions for polarity and format checks, freeze 8 for calibration and 8 for exploratory evaluation by a prespecified split, and keep those exploratory results separate from future protected evidence. Budget at most **20–30 minutes**, mostly model startup. Run zero fresh solver completions. If archived question-only and prefix scores can already answer the ranking/calibration question, skip this job. Advance a candidate only if the raw ranking separates benefiting cases, its cost-adjusted advantage survives the cheap controls, and the result is stable across seed or draw halves. A result from eight explored evaluation questions can stop a weak candidate. A positive result earns protected validation.
5. **Conditional protected confirmation.** If the diagnostic justifies fresh labels, start with **12 new held-out questions × two caps × eight draws = 192 solves** for 4K→8K. Use shared question and seed schedules. For an in-flight prediction, branch short and long from identical eligible prefixes using the existing continuation harness. Log early closures separately. For admission, use independent full solves at each cap. Record beneficial and harmful transitions without enforcing monotonicity, and cluster estimates by question. The earlier 192-solve L40S grid took 2h 14m. With the higher mean cap in this narrower design, plan **about 3 L40S-hours**, conditional on measured throughput and realized lengths. This is a directional confirmation with wide intervals. Decide whether to size a larger test using observed variance and break-even prediction cost. Do not scale from an inconclusive weak effect.

**Decision enabled:** A good raw ordering with poor calibrated probabilities supports calibrating one cheap score. An advantage only after adding early features supports a focused checkpoint test. Neither pattern, or no advantage once overhead is charged, supports shifting the effort to load-aware scheduling. This review authorized no training, serving changes or GPU execution.
