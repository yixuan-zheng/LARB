# Workload implications for serving scaling

## What the adaptive screen tells us

The Qwen3-4B screen used 24 training questions and 33 held-out short-answer Omni-MATH questions. Each set had equal representation across three source difficulty strata. On held-out questions, fixed Low, Medium, and High solved 10, 11, and 22 of 33. High was the only successful mode on 10 questions. Twelve of its 22 successes were also solved in a cheaper mode, and nine questions failed in all three modes. The trained policy chose High 28 times, Medium five times, and Low zero times. Its High frequency barely changed across the three strata (9/11, 10/11, and 9/11).

The workload favored High overall, but it still offered meaningful opportunities to save compute. In a retrospective replay that preserves each fixed-High success and picks the cheapest compatible observed completion, charged tokens drop by about 56% before routing overhead (239,093 fixed-High tokens versus 106,249 hindsight tokens across 33 questions). The rule can spend less on fixed-High failures as well as use a cheaper successful mode on fixed-High successes. This is an optimistic, single-draw calculation with access to all outcomes. It measures apparent headroom in this sample, and it cannot establish learnability or expected savings. Seven of the twelve High successes shared with cheaper modes came from the easiest stratum. Source difficulty helped describe the cohort, but it did not cleanly identify each question's effort requirement.

The tested router fell below both the shuffled control and the fixed-mode mixture. The sample cannot separate workload composition from training limitations: one seed, 22 updates, 24 training prompts, and one greedy generation per question and mode at evaluation. The trial did not measure cross-domain generalization or payoff from a larger training run. Close adaptive training with those limitations recorded.

## Multi-turn agent hypothesis

We hypothesized that a turn's role and observable state could make reasoning requirements easier to identify. For example, an obvious lookup with known arguments may need little deliberation, while conflicting user constraints or recovery after a tool error may benefit from more. These signals could vary across turns within a task. We did not measure their predictive value or compare it with math.

The local τ²-bench work established interface compatibility on controlled fixtures. Qwen3-4B produced structured tool calls under 512- and 2,048-token checks. At the binding 2,048-token cap, it returned the expected tool arguments and a call ID, and it handled the mock tool result. Fixed-history airline and retail replays also exposed customer-fidelity failures in both local simulator sizes. That blocked trustworthy full-episode outcome measurement.

We did not compare agent task completion across budgets, label which turns benefited from extra effort, or evaluate routing against cheap turn-level baselines. We also did not measure whether reasoning requirements are easier to identify on agents than on math. If agent evaluation resumes, first qualify the customer or use verified reference histories. Then compare repeated fixed-cap outcomes on identical turn histories before using full episodes to measure downstream effects. This is a separate roadmap item. Serving scaling takes priority.

## A candidate mixed serving workload

Use GSM8K, MATH, and Omni-MATH as sources for a separate workload variant. Preserve the existing AIME workload as a distinct stress case. Start with a roughly equal source mix during qualification. Within each source, use disjoint calibration questions and repeated draws from the pinned solver at the **actual serving caps** (4K and 8K for the current policy). Estimate realized tokens, deadline-relevant service time, natural reasoning closure, and the change in verified correctness. Look for three practically different groups:

- Short-solvable requests that save meaningful realized output and service time at the lower cap.
- Budget-sensitive requests that need more effort often enough to expose the accuracy cost of cuts.
- Low-success or unstable requests, including cases where extra effort provides little gain or harms outcomes.

Dataset names are recruitment strata. Use measured responses to qualify each group and report the mix. If GSM8K often closes naturally before 4K, it can supply realistic short requests, while contributing little to the measured effect of cutting from 8K to 4K. Keep the held-out replay disjoint from qualification and freeze the mixture, grader, seeds, arrivals, and deadline before comparison. Use the same trace across policies. Report overall and per-stratum correct-on-time goodput, accuracy, realized tokens, deadline misses, and interventions. State explicitly if the workload intentionally enriches budget-sensitive questions, so its aggregate result is interpreted for that mix.

This workload proposal leaves the existing AIME study and frozen pilot validity rules intact. It requires no additional adaptive training.

## Final CPU diagnostic · 96 mixed-workload questions

The saved step-24 evaluation contains one forced completion per question at each cap. Medium and High both succeeded on 68 questions, both failed on 16, High alone succeeded on 10, and Medium alone succeeded on two. A hindsight policy starting from Medium could keep its 70 successes and add the ten High-only successes, reaching 80/96 on these draws. This is an observed upper bound for the two actions with known outcomes.

The router selected High on 38 questions. It captured four of the ten High-only successes, selected High on both Medium-only successes, and made 32 additional High choices on questions with equal observed correctness. Within-source shuffling of the same mode counts would capture 4.56 High-only successes and incur 1.00 Medium-only regression on average. Learned routing thus gained two answers over fixed Medium, while the shuffle gains an expected 3.56. The learned router spent 112,027 additional solver tokens on the 34 High selections with no observed correctness gain, relative to their paired Medium completions. Routing prefill is excluded from this total.

Early termination limits the cost of choosing High. For 47/96 forced High completions, including 9/38 router-selected High completions, all output-side tokens fit below 2,048. For 77/96 and 23/38, respectively, output-side tokens fit below 8,192. These are conservative counts of reasoning ending below those ceilings because the upper bounds include the mode prefix and final answer. The compact archive omits termination reasons and exact reasoning lengths, so the counts do not distinguish natural closure from malformed or failed completions. Only 1/38 selected High completions used no more total solver tokens than its paired Medium completion.

For serving, learned assignment needs to concentrate higher effort on requests where it changes correctness and where the additional work can finish before the deadline. It can also help by avoiding expensive extensions that delay other requests. The mixed workload had a limited but real opportunity on the archived draws. The learned policy did not capture it better than the source-matched control, and the live results did not show a consistent incremental deadline-goodput advantage over shuffle. The central learned-routing hypothesis was not validated for the tested model, data and training setup. These observations do not settle whether multi-turn state provides stronger signals. The agent fixtures established tool-call compatibility only; we have no paired within-turn benefit or episode-level evidence.

## How the predictor experiments and GRPO fit together

The final diagnostic quantifies the same allocation problem that motivated the earlier predictors. Fixed-cap grids and repeated continuation draws had already shown that more reasoning helps some requests, does little for others, and sometimes changes a correct answer to a wrong one. The 56% hindsight savings from the earlier 33-question screen and the 80/96 Medium–High hindsight accuracy above are two views of that headroom, on different cohorts and under different objectives. Both give the allocator access to observed outcomes. Neither shows how much of the opportunity is learnable before a request finishes.

The explicit predictors tried to rank the gain from more reasoning. In the held-out Omni-MATH admission replay at roughly 6,959 tokens per request, length-only 4K failure risk reached 44.8% accuracy, compared with 43.4% for prefill correctness gain and 42.7% for gain per added token. The larger protected checkpoint probe estimated a 2.24-point aggregate lift over prompt-only at the same extension count, with an interval crossing zero and 46.1% within-question concordance. That checkpoint population contained only requests still reasoning at 4K. Together, these results showed that budget sensitivity can coexist with little reliable signal about which individual request to extend. Short-mode failure risk is an imperfect proxy because it includes requests that more reasoning will not rescue.

GRPO changed how the policy learned. It trained Qwen3-4B to choose a reasoning mode and generate within it using answer verification and mode-specific length rewards. It did not require a label for the right budget. The underlying allocation test remained the same. High must be assigned where it yields useful additional correctness, and expensive choices elsewhere must be avoided. The mixed-workload router learned a source-level pattern, choosing High for 6/32 GSM8K, 12/32 MATH, and 20/32 Omni-MATH questions. Within sources, it caught four of ten observed High-only successes versus 4.56 expected from shuffling the same mode counts, and assigned High to both Medium-only successes. Most High placements left observed correctness unchanged while still adding tokens. This is direct evidence that the learned policy failed to improve question-level allocation on the archived draws.

The training reward also omitted deadline slack and the queueing cost of longer work on other requests. That leaves a second hurdle for serving even if a mode choice helps eventual correctness. Live combined routing sometimes improved on fixed or load-only policies, but the within-source shuffle often matched or beat it on deadline goodput. The evidence identifies where the tested policy fell short. It cannot determine whether the main cause was limited training scale, weak question-level signals, or the mismatch between a per-answer reward and a shared deadline objective.

The final conclusion is that allocation headroom was established, while a consistent advantage from learned question-specific routing was not validated. Close the tested prediction and training branches. Retain the shuffled-plus-load and fixed-budget controls in serving scaling, with multi-turn state as an explicitly untested hypothesis.

## Final comparison · deployable probabilistic allocation

Using only the 24 development questions, select the least expensive expected Medium/High mixture on a 0.1 probability grid whose expected accuracy is within five points of fixed High. The frozen result is **40% High and 60% Medium**. Deployment can draw once per request and retain the draw on retries. The policy uses no source labels, evaluation-question roster, or routing prefill.

On 96 separate held-out questions, expected accuracy and mean solver tokens were 76.25% and 2,594 for the global coin, 76.63% and 2,852 for the within-source shuffle, and 75.0% and 3,161 for learned routing before its mean 109-token routing prefill. Prespecified seed 151 gave 74/96 and 2,377 tokens. Fixed Medium and High anchor the range at 72.92%/1,776 and 81.25%/3,821. Each forced-mode outcome comes from one saved draw. The coin's expected result is a mixture of fixed modes and cannot establish a deadline or queueing gain.
