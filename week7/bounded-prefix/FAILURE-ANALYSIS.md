# Why the bounded-prefix reader missed gains

This is a retrospective review of the six missed gains at 28 extensions. It joins the sealed rankings and reader notes to the full saved prefixes, both branch outputs, and the existing Week 6 text features. Later text explains what happened after the fork. Every checkpoint judgment below uses only the question and the specified prefix view. No new inference or signal fitting was performed.

| Missed gain (rank of 57) | Evidence at 4K and what changed |
|---|---|
| q25-b0 (30) | The window contains both the substitution and its dropped factor of two, which gives a constant of 312 instead of 156. The long path corrects the constant and finishes the existing route. The reader forecast a gain but ranked it below the cutoff. |
| q18-b1 (34) | The window circles around column digit-pair sums. The short answer counts the pairs independently and misses the second constraint. The long path enforces that constraint and reduces the count to a row digit-sum problem. The reader forecast a gain but gave many no-gain cases higher scores. |

At this operating point, 10 of the 28 selected cases stayed wrong and 16 were correct in both branches. The median Week 6 recent four-gram repetition was 0.326 for the six misses and 0.329 for the ten selected persistent failures. Median correction-marker counts were 10 and 10.5. These are existing descriptive proxies with their original windows, which differ from the 512-token reader input. q29-b0 shows productive correction and q18-b0 shows recovery with zero recent correction markers. An unfinished step or a correction can be real without predicting that more tokens will fix the answer. In selected persistent failure q11-b1, the reader treated an unresolved counting step as evidence the continuation could finish. In the top-ranked q19-b0, it recognized the already-derived correct value and forecast a gain anyway.

The existing explicit numerical answer-claim heuristic found no hits in the full prefixes of the six misses. The two branches provide one realized outcome each. They do not supply repeated comparable answer probes over time, so these traces support observations about changing derivations and terminal answers, with answer instability itself unmeasured.

**Attribution.** The short window demonstrably omits useful setup in q14-b1 and q29-b0 and an earlier solution route in q10-b1. In q25-b0 and q18-b0, the reader already had the relevant local evidence. Archived full-prefix readers captured all eight gains at 28 extensions, but they used different readers, two-reader averaging, and a different forecast elicitation. That comparison cannot estimate the effect of window length alone. Some local notes mistake unfinished visible reasoning for a wrong forced answer, or treat a conceivable later fix as evidence that it will happen. This is consistent with an elicitation problem. The evidence notes followed the scored outcome token, so they can illustrate errors but cannot establish what caused the score. The same-reader comparison establishes that adding this particular window degraded ranking for this model and prompt on these cases. It cannot isolate reader capability from prompt design. Distinct stochastic suffixes also limit what can be inferred from any single checkpoint.

**Decision.** Keep the STOP decision. There is no trace-based case here for a repetition rule, a correction-marker rule, or an answer-instability claim. The unresolved design question is whether a reader can identify a feasible remaining step tied to an unmet requirement and distinguish it from an already-answerable state. These explored cases describe that distinction but cannot validate a new signal. Any further diagnostic would need to vary one factor at a time with the same reader and scoring procedure, followed by protected validation for a generalization claim.
